We sell AI agents for a living. And yet, most of the time, customers choose us because of the humans on our sales and success team.

That sounds like a contradiction, so I’ve been trying to unpack it—partly because it matters commercially, but mostly because it feels like a preview of where a lot of work is going.

I think the simplest version is this: AI can produce “good enough” output quickly, but people don’t buy output. They buy trust. And trust is still strangely human.

### The “human feel” problem

Inside our team, we run a bunch of internal agents in Slack. They do real work: pulling data, drafting first passes, helping with operational tasks. But something interesting happens in adoption.

When the agent feels like “a tool,” people use it occasionally.

When the agent feels like “a colleague,” people use it constantly.

That doesn’t mean pretending it’s human in a deceptive way. It means giving it cues that match how humans already coordinate:

- it acknowledges you (the equivalent of “saw this”)
- it signals when something will take time
- it behaves consistently enough that you can build expectations

It’s a small thing, but it changes the relationship. People stop asking “is this worth trying?” and start assuming “this is part of how we work.”

The same dynamic shows up with our customers. The more they make their customer-facing agent sound like a person, the happier their shoppers are. Some shoppers don’t even realize it’s AI. Sometimes you’ll see a message like “thank you ma’am, you’re so kind,” and you realize how easily humans fill in the blanks.

Then the spell breaks.

### What happens when people realize it’s AI

The uncomfortable part is that “human feel” seems to matter most right up until the moment people are reminded it’s not human.

There are studies that suggest people can rate AI replies highly—sometimes even higher than human replies—until they are told it was AI. Then the exact same text is suddenly worth less.

That maps pretty well to how I feel day-to-day right now. We’re entering a world where we assume most text might be generated. And once you start assuming that, you start discounting it.

It’s not even always rational. The work might be good. The reasoning might be solid. But something in your brain goes, “this was cheap,” so it feels less trustworthy.

I’ve felt this in team communication too: when someone consistently sends AI-generated messages, I catch myself trusting them slightly less—even if the content is fine. Not because AI is bad, but because it’s hard to know what they actually thought through versus what they forwarded.

It creates a weird incentive loop:

- you’re expected to use AI because it makes you faster
- you’re subtly punished if people think you’re using AI too much

### The “law of shitty clickthroughs”, but for AI

This reminds me of an old idea from growth: every channel gets worse once everyone uses it.

At first, a channel is high-signal. Early adopters get attention because the medium is novel and the bar is low.

Then everyone piles in. The volume increases. The average quality drops. Audiences adapt by ignoring more of it. The channel decays.

AI text feels like it’s entering that phase right now. Not because AI can’t be good, but because the default output is abundant and average. When “average” becomes infinite, humans develop new filters. We become stingy with attention.

So even if you use AI well, you’re swimming in the same pool as AI slop. People can’t easily tell the difference at a glance, so they assume the median.

### So why do humans still win?

If you’re selling AI, it’s tempting to think the “human” part is temporary. That once the tech improves, the human advantage disappears.

Maybe.

But right now, the human advantage isn’t just capability. It’s intent.

When a human says, “I looked into this,” you can infer effort, taste, and accountability. You might disagree, but you believe they did work.

When an AI says, “I looked into this,” you don’t know what that means. Did it skim? Did it verify? Did it hallucinate? Did it optimize for sounding confident? It might be right, but the social contract is different.

That’s why, for now, having real people to talk to can be a differentiator—even for an AI company. It signals that there is someone accountable on the other side. Someone who will notice when the output is wrong, and someone who will care enough to fix it.

### How I’m trying to use this insight

For some things, AI output being “cheap” is fine. Sales emails, internal summaries, first drafts—places where the reader is not looking for a love letter.

For other things, I’m going slower. I’d rather publish less, but have it feel like someone made it and cared.

That’s not a moral stance. It’s a bet on attention. It’s a bet that “human-made” is becoming a scarce signal again.

And if I’m wrong, I’ll update. But for now, the most interesting part of selling AI is that it keeps teaching me what humans actually value.
