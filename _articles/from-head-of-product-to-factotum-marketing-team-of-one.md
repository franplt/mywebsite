About a month ago, I went from Head of Product at TextYess to “factotum” (or Head of Marketing, if you like) after we hired Antoine Pirard as my product replacement.

On paper, it’s a simple story: new product leader joins, I move to marketing. In practice, it’s a full reset of identity and operating rhythm. Product work has a cadence. Marketing is everything, all the time, in public, with no clear definition of “done.”

### The disorienting part

The first surprise was how quickly the “list of things to do” became ridiculous.

I’ve worked in marketing before, but the landscape has changed a lot. In 2020, you could be good at a few channels and build momentum. Now, marketing is a stack of systems: ads, creative, web, SEO, sales enablement, lifecycle, partnerships, events, analytics, positioning, messaging, content, PR, internal comms… and each of those has its own tooling and its own “minimum viable competence.”

When you’re a team of one, you touch all of it.

And because you touch all of it, the list only grows. Nobody is deciding what you’re not doing this quarter. You have to decide that, alone, while the inbound requests keep coming.

### The exciting part

At the same time, it’s been genuinely exciting.

There’s something addictive about shipping across many surfaces in a short time window. One day you’re writing a launch plan. The next you’re reshaping the website. The next you’re setting up a new ad account. The next you’re assembling a deck for sales.

You get to see the company from a different angle: what people misunderstand, what they repeat, what gets lost between teams, what customers actually respond to.

It also forces you to develop taste. When you’re not surrounded by specialists, you can’t hide behind “that’s not my area.” You ship, learn, ship again.

### Where AI actually helps (and where it doesn’t)

I’ve been leaning heavily on AI tools to make this survivable. Not as a replacement for thinking, but as a way to keep moving when the workload is wider than your skill set.

The biggest value has been in compressing the “first draft” phase:

- outlining a page before I touch the website
- turning scattered notes into a coherent narrative
- generating variations of copy so I can choose, not invent from scratch
- doing basic research and summarization so I’m not starting cold
- stitching together an initial plan for something I’ve never done before

It’s not glamorous work, but it’s the work that otherwise blocks you.

There are also places where AI makes things worse if you’re not careful:

- when it encourages you to publish more than you can stand behind
- when it produces confident but generic messaging that feels empty
- when it saves time up front but creates rework later because the foundation is wrong

In marketing, the quality bar is emotional. People can feel “this was made fast.” Sometimes speed is fine. Sometimes it kills trust.

### The operational challenge: context switching

The hardest part hasn’t been any single task. It’s the constant switching.

Product work rewards depth. Marketing, especially solo, rewards breadth. You might spend an hour on a PR announcement, then jump into ad creative, then jump into analytics, then jump into a sales deck, then jump into website copy.

Each switch has a tax:

- you lose the thread
- you reopen old decisions
- you second-guess the strategy

I’ve started to treat “context” as a first-class asset. If I make a decision, I write down why. If I learn something from sales calls, I capture it. If a message lands well, I keep it. Otherwise, you end up re-deriving your own thinking every week.

### A rule I’m trying to live by

When you’re a team of one, you can’t do everything. The only way out is to choose an anchor and let everything else earn its place.

For me, the anchor has been building a clearer system to track what’s working and why—so I’m not just doing “a lot of marketing,” I’m doing marketing that compounds.

That changes the question from “can I do this?” to “does this move the thing we actually care about?”

It also makes it easier to say no. Or at least to say “not now.”

### What I don’t know yet

I’m still early in the role, and I don’t want to pretend I’ve cracked it.

I don’t fully know what the right balance is between:

- fast shipping vs. careful craft
- breadth vs. focus
- automation vs. human judgment

I also don’t yet know what parts of the workload should become a system, and what parts should become a person. “Team of one” is a phase, not an ideal.

But the direction is clear: if I want this to be sustainable, I need to build a simple operating system for marketing—one that preserves decisions, reduces switching costs, and makes it obvious what we’re not doing.

Right now, it’s exhausting. And at the same time, it’s the most energized I’ve felt at work in a long time.
